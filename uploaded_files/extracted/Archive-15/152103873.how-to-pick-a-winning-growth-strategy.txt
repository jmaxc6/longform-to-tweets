In today’s fast-paced and competitive business landscape, consistent growth is a necessity. With countless strategies available, deciding how to scale your business can feel daunting. Should you dive into paid advertising to drive instant purchases, invest in search engine optimization (SEO) for long-term organic traffic, harness the power of social media to engage your audience, implement sales automation to connect with prospects 1-on-1, or leverage influencer partnerships to expand your reach? Choosing a strategy that fits your goals, resources, and audience is critical.

#1 - Getting your first ten customers
Company stage matters, and the growth (i.e. sales and marketing) strategies to acquire one’s first ten customers differ from those employed at later stages. If you are a B2B business, the best approach involves promoting your product to your network, asking for referrals, posting in online communities, and attending relevant networking events, as 
this article
 highlights.
Credit: Lenny’s Newsletter
If you are a B2C business, all of the above still works well, but I suggest two additional strategies. First, go where your target audience hangs out, whether online or offline. This might involve identifying relevant sub-Reddits or X.com circles or finding local clubs or events your target audience will likely attend. Second, post physical placements such as flyers, stickers, and signs at these offline locations. 
If you have not yet acquired your first ten customers, this is not the time to think of systems, scalability, and predictable revenue, but rather to “
Do things that don’t scale
,” as YC founder and startup expert Paul Graham advises.
This actually works better than you’d expect
#2 - The time vs. money matrix
Once you have acquired at least ten customers, the next step is assessing the time vs. money matrix (below). When referring to time and money in this context, I am referring specifically to the time and money that a team is willing to commit to growth efforts. If a team has millions in cash but has a cap of $1k/month for growth efforts, that is “low money.” Similarly, if a team has tons of time but is tied up in priorities outside of growth, that is “low time.” 
The time vs. money matrix
This step is important because time or money can be serious limiting factors that may rule out certain growth strategies. If you want to outcompete your competitors on paid ads but do not have the budget earmarked for it, you are not going to get much traction. If you want to outshine competitors by writing handwritten notes to prospective customers but do not have the time, you are better off finding an alternative strategy.
What is a reasonable benchmark for “high time” and “high money”? Based on my experience, some decent benchmarks are as follows:
Money
High = $25k+/month
Medium = $10k+/month
Low = < $3k/month
Time
High = 20 hours per week
Medium = 10 hours per week
Low = < 3 hours per week
If you want to succeed with your growth efforts, you must be at least ‘Medium’ on one of these. Being ‘Medium’ on money lets you pay for software or hire talent to handle much of the work. Being ‘Medium’ on time enables you to compensate for lack of funds through hustle and effort.
If you are not at least ‘Medium’ on one of the dimensions, you need to either increase money (e.g. by doing a fundraise) or increase time (e.g. hiring someone or re-prioritizing). While many startup founders feel overwhelmed and out of their depth when leading growth efforts, handling it internally is necessary if you lack the money to outsource it.
#3 - Research the competition 
Now that you have acquired your first ten customers and are at least a ‘Medium’ on either time or money, the next step is to start researching your competitor’s growth strategies. I would suggest picking at least five close competitors in your niche, ideally all of whom are a few steps ahead of you (but not too far ahead; a billion-dollar company’s strategies are not easily replicable).
Learning from the best 
Why focus so much on the competition? Because choosing a strong growth strategy involves a huge number of factors: your niche, target audience, price point, company resources, etc. Trying to reinvent the wheel and operate entirely from first principles is highly likely to lead you astray, but by benchmarking against your best competitors, you can ensure that your game plan is battle-tested.

Considerations When Copying Competitors
It is worth stating that just because your competitors utilize specific growth strategies does not mean they are the best fit for your business, for a few reasons. First, your competitors may be optimizing for different things. For example, you may be seeking to maximize profit while they are pursuing revenue growth at all costs, even if it is unprofitable in the short term. Second, compared to your competitors, you may have vastly different resources.
Third, your competitors may have an appetite for legally questionable tactics or strategies you are unwilling to stomach. Having lived in San Francisco for 6 years and seen the inside operations of many startups, I have learned that most successful early-to-mid-stage startups look at almost all decisions purely through the lens of risk vs. reward. Nothing is off-limits, and if you told the founder, “You could get sued for this”, you will be told something like, “Settling the lawsuit is worth it given how profitable this is”. You may need a different approach if you do not have the same risk appetite.
Don’t let your competitors drag you down with them
#4 - Picking a Winning Growth Strategy
Now that we have analyzed competitors’ growth strategies and have an understanding of which are the most popular and worth imitating, the final step is to go through two final checklists to determine if either paid ads or SEO are a potentially poor fit:
Paid Ads:
While the fully fleshed-out checklist can be found in 
this article
, which is the best place to go for clarification on any of these points, I will still re-print the key points below:
#1 - Are you in a sensitive industry (e.g., adult content, gambling, pharmaceuticals)?
#2 - Is your product or service sufficiently high price or high-margin?
#3 - Do you have sufficient budget?
#4 - Are you able to handle uncertainty and make calculated bets?
#5 - Are your desired customers easy to find online?
Especially if you are a firm no for any of #2 - #5, there are better fits for your brand than paid ads.
Some industries are much better for paid ads than others
SEO:
While there are some other SEO research tools out there (e.g. 
Moz
 and 
Ubersuggest
), I would highly recommend paying for either 
Ahrefs
 or 
SEMRush
 for your SEO research and ongoing management:
#1 - When using either SEMRush or Ahrefs and searching for niche-relevant keywords, is there at least 10,000 cumulative search volume for keywords with keyword difficulty (KD) under 30?
#2 - Are you willing to wait at least 6 months to start seeing a solid boost in traffic from SEO?
#3 - Are you willing to wait at least 12 months to start seeing consistent purchases driven by SEO?
While results can and do happen faster, these are reasonable benchmarks for what you should expect to wait if you are serious about SEO.
